Nobel Prize in the Clinic

The Nobel Prize in the Clinic117 Nobel Prizes in Physiology or Medicine, and how long each took to reach patients

Hee Seung Nam, MD, Orthopedic Surgeon · November 25, 2026

The Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded 117 times from 1901 to 2026. Where are those discoveries in the clinic today? Some became tests and drugs we use every day; others became the principles behind care. A few are still waiting at the laboratory door, and a few reached patients and then disappeared. Looking through an orthopedic surgeon's eyes, I sorted the 117 prizes into four groups and measured the years from discovery to first patient to prize.

This timeline is built from the appendix and chapter timelines of my forthcoming book The Nobel Prize That Arrived in the Clinic (in Korean). The four groups reflect my own judgment. One chapter is adapted in the column The Nobel Prize inside a joint injection.

117prizes · 1901–2026
Arrived41Became a drug, test, procedure or vaccine used in the clinic.
Foundation69Not a tool we use directly, but it became part of how medicine is understood.
Not yet4Awarded, but there is no treatment for patients yet, or it is still in trials.
Abandoned4Reached the clinic once, then proved wrong or dangerous and disappeared.

1949 is counted twice: one laureate's work became foundation, the other's was abandoned.

What stands out

  • Patients often came first. Of the 47 discoveries in the chart, 31 were already being used in patients before the prize arrived. Insulin won the year after its first patient; cortisone, two years after.
  • In 8 cases the prize came first. The first RNA interference drug arrived 12 years after the prize, and the first telomerase drug 15 years after.
  • The longest wait belongs to nitroglycerin. In 1998, 119 years after it was first reported as a drug for angina, the principle of why it works (nitric oxide) won the prize.
  • The prizes still waiting are all recent. Autophagy, microRNA, regulatory T cells and optogenetics, the four that have not reached the clinic, were all awarded in 2016 or later. The distance to the clinic only shows with time.

How to read this, and sources

  • “First used in patients” is when the discovery was first used in the clinic. For vascular suture I followed finger replantation, as the book does; for hepatitis B, hepatitis C and HIV, the screening of donated blood.
  • When only a period is known (for example, the 1910s), the point sits in the middle of that period; the expanded note gives the original period.
  • Laureates and prize citations follow the Nobel Foundation; the short summaries are my own.
