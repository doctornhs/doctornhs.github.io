Knee injections: how often is safe?

Hee Seung Nam, MD, Orthopedic Surgeon · November 21, 2026

After I give a knee injection, I almost always hear two questions. “When can I have the next one?” And, from patients with diabetes, “I heard it raises blood sugar. Is that all right?”

In an earlier column, “The Nobel Prize inside a joint injection”, I wrote about where this injection came from. This time I will answer with numbers: how many months apart is safe, how many times a year, and how much blood sugar rises and for how many days to be careful.

The short answer first

  • For the same knee, the usual advice is at least three months apart and no more than three or four times a year.
  • The “at least two weeks apart” in the drug label is a minimum, not a recommended interval.
  • If the relief lasts less than a month, it is time to look for the cause again, not to inject more often.
  • If you have diabetes, check your blood sugar more often for three days after the injection.
  • If knee replacement is planned, avoid an injection in the three months before surgery.

First, find out which injection you had

Knee injections come in three broad kinds: a steroid injection that quickly calms inflammation (in Korea often called the “bone shot”), a hyaluronic acid injection that adds lubrication to the joint fluid (often called the “cartilage shot”), and injections made from your own blood such as PRP. The intervals and the blood sugar issue in this column apply to steroid injections.

The problem is that many people never learn which one they received. It is easy to hear only “an inflammation shot” or “a pain shot.” The itemized bill lists the drug names, so if you see triamcinolone, dexamethasone or methylprednisolone, that was a steroid. Writing down the dates, including injections at other clinics, helps a great deal at your next visit.

How long does it work?

A steroid injection works for a shorter time than most people expect. In a Cochrane review that pooled 27 trials (1,767 people) in knee osteoarthritis, pain relief was clearest one to two weeks after the injection, small to moderate at four to six weeks, small at 13 weeks (three months), and there was no evidence of any effect left at six months.1

The guidelines of the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) and the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) also recommend this injection for short-term pain relief.2,3 So what one injection can buy is a few weeks, at most two or three months, of comfort. The best use of that window is to start exercising.

Why “three months”?

The Korean label for triamcinolone says joint injections should, in principle, be given at least two weeks apart.4 That is a floor, not a target. The rule you usually hear in clinic is at least three months apart and no more than three or four times a year for the same joint, and there are three reasons for it.

First, regular injections every three months do not make things better. In a 2017 trial published in JAMA, 140 patients with knee osteoarthritis received either 40 mg of triamcinolone or saline every 12 weeks for two years. The steroid group had no more pain relief than the saline group, and their cartilage thickness on MRI decreased slightly more (an average of 0.21 mm versus 0.10 mm).5

Second, the joint occasionally deteriorates quickly after an injection. A review of 459 knee and hip injections given in 2018 at a US university hospital found joint problems confirmed on imaging in 8% (36 patients). The most common was faster-than-expected narrowing of the joint space, and there were also small fractures in the bone under the cartilage, bone death and rapid joint destruction.6 This does not prove the injection caused them, but it does mean the injection is not free of consequences.

Third, the effect itself rarely lasts beyond three months, as the Cochrane review showed.

So “three months” does not mean “have one every three months.” It is closer to “even if it hurts, do not repeat it within three months.”

If the relief keeps getting shorter

If an injection that once gave three good months now gives two, then one, your knee is sending a signal. The drug has not become weaker; more likely the inside of the joint has changed. This is the time not to inject more often but to look at the joint again with an X-ray and, if needed, an MRI; to rework strengthening exercise, weight control and oral medication; and, if the cartilage is badly worn, to discuss the next step, including surgery. The ACR guideline also lists exercise, tai chi and self-management programs as first-line treatment.3

If knee replacement is planned

An injection shortly before knee replacement is linked to the risk of infection afterwards. In a meta-analysis by a Korean group that pooled 14 studies and about 370,000 patients, a steroid injection within three months before surgery raised the risk of infection of the artificial joint about 1.26-fold, while widening the window to six months showed no statistically clear difference.7

That is why, when a date for knee replacement is set, the first thing to check is when the last injection was. Please mention injections given at other clinics too.

If you have diabetes: watch the three days after the injection

Part of a steroid injected into a joint is absorbed into the rest of the body and raises blood sugar. In a systematic review of seven studies (72 patients) in people with diabetes, every study showed a rise in blood sugar after the injection, in some cases as high as 500 mg/dL. The rise usually came not immediately but between 24 and 72 hours afterwards.8

According to a summary in American Family Physician, blood sugar peaked between 2 and 84 hours after the injection and took anywhere from 18 hours to 21 days to return to its usual level.9 Most of the people studied had well-controlled diabetes, so those whose diabetes is poorly controlled need to be even more careful.

If you have diabetes and are having a knee injection

  • Before the injection: tell your doctor that you have diabetes and your most recent HbA1c. If your blood sugar has been swinging widely, it may be better to postpone the injection or try something else first.
  • For three days afterwards: check fasting and after-meal blood sugar more often than usual. Days one to three matter most.
  • Do not change your medication on your own: do not stop or increase tablets or insulin by yourself; if high readings continue, talk to the doctor who manages your diabetes. If you use insulin, it helps to discuss this before the injection.
  • Contact a doctor promptly if: your blood sugar stays far above your usual range, or you have severe thirst, frequent urination or weakness. And, separately from blood sugar, if the injected knee becomes increasingly swollen and hot with a fever (rare, but it can be a joint infection).

In closing

The short answer to “how often is safe?” is “at least three months apart for the same knee, and no more than three or four times a year.” But what I watch more closely in clinic is not the interval: it is how many weeks of relief this injection gave. That number decides the next step.

Use the weeks an injection buys to start exercising, and if those weeks keep shrinking, look at the knee again instead of adding injections. That is how this injection can be used safely for a long time.

This article is general medical information. The interval and number of injections can differ with the condition of your knee, other illnesses and your future treatment plans, so please confirm with your doctor.
