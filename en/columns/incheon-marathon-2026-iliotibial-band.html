The Incheon Marathon again, one year later: what my splits and my knee told me

Hee Seung Nam, MD, Orthopedic Surgeon · November 23, 2026

One year later, I stood at the start line of the Incheon Marathon again. I remembered last year clearly. 3:49:13. The number on the result sheet was not bad, but the race my body remembers had already ended at 30 km. My legs turned to lead, and my pace fell into the mid-6-minute range and then into the 7s. That was my memory of the wall.

This year, crossing the finish line, I saw 3:48:04. One minute and nine seconds faster than last year.

I pushed the wall back 5 km

The most welcome change was where the wall came. Last year I ran out of energy and fell apart just past 30 km. This year I held a 5-minute pace all the way to 35 km (5'57" for 30 to 35 km). The wall moved from 30 km to the 36 to 40 km stretch, exactly 5 km later.

There was no special secret. Running a little every morning and building leg strength pushed the cruelest part of the marathon back by 5 km.

But the old weakness remains: a weak finish

I also have to face my weakness honestly: I lack strength at the end.

Perhaps it is my habit of running short distances of around 5 km at 4'40" to 5'00" per km. When the gun goes off, I feel light and step on the gas without realizing it. This year, too, because the course was flat and pleasant, I ran the first 15 km at an unwise 4'38" to 4'48" per km (4'41" on average).

My first half took 1:40:57, more than a minute and a half faster than last year, but the bill came due after 35 km. My pace for the 5 km from 35 to 40 km was 6'52" per km. I paid back in the second half, as debt, the energy I had not saved in the first. My men's rank went from 1,194th at 5 km to 2,349th at the finish. Being passed by more than 1,000 runners was the longest, loneliest stretch of the 42.195 km.

Still, I want to give myself credit for picking my feet up again to 6'07" per km over the last 2.2 km and not giving up. This race made my current position clear. Judging by my aerobic capacity and early speed, I am a 3:30-something runner, but fat metabolism and muscular endurance after 35 km are still homework.

The day after the finish, what stayed with me most was the outside of my knee

I have written a lot about times, but what I felt most over the past two days was something else. The pain was at the small bump on the front-outer side of the top of the shin, below the outside of the knee. This is Gerdy's tubercle, where the iliotibial band attaches to the tibia.

Straightening my knee after sitting, or taking the first step after being still, brought severe pain.

Sore thighs and calves always come with a full marathon. This time, though, this one spot stood out more clearly than anywhere else. As an orthopedic surgeon, I have explained this area countless times in clinic. This time I am learning it through my own knee.

What the iliotibial band is, and why that spot hurt

The iliotibial (IT) band is a long, tough band that starts at the pelvis, runs down the outside of the thigh and attaches to the tibia below the knee. It is not a muscle but thick fascia, pulled from above by the tensor fasciae latae and gluteus maximus. When running, it holds the outside of the knee steady every time the foot lands.

At a typical 170 to 180 steps per minute, 3 hours and 48 minutes means close to 40,000 foot strikes, both feet together. With each of those landings, the IT band braced the outside of the knee and pulled on its attachment to the tibia. My judgment is this: overuse of the IT band caused small injuries to the fibers at its tibial attachment, and the inflammation that followed showed up as pain whenever I straightened the knee or started to move.

Most people link iliotibial band syndrome with pain higher up, near the bump on the outside of the femur (the lateral epicondyle). It used to be explained as friction from the band sliding back and forth over that bump. Anatomical studies, however, show that the band is firmly anchored to the femur by fibrous strands and does not roll over the bump, and that compression of the fat layer beneath it, rich in nerves and blood vessels, may be the source of pain. In my case, the load seems to have concentrated lower down, at the end where the band attaches to bone.

A weak finish and knee pain may share the same root

Putting my splits next to my knee, the two seemed connected. A study of distance runners with iliotibial band syndrome found weaker hip abductors on the painful side, and symptoms improved as that strength improved with six weeks of rehabilitation.

When the outer hip muscles tire, the pelvis drops toward the other side with each landing and the thigh drifts inward. That pulls the IT band on the outside of the knee tighter. During the stretch after 35 km when my pace collapsed, my hip muscles were probably exhausted too. The collapsed pace and the next day's knee pain may be two faces of the same homework.

If the outside of your knee hurts after a marathon

This is the recovery sequence I recommend to runners in clinic. This time it is my turn to follow it myself.

  • The first few days: rest from running. Ice the sore spot and, if needed, take an anti-inflammatory for a short time. Do not rub it hard or force a stretch.
  • Once the pain settles: start hip abductor exercises, such as side-lying leg raises, clamshells and side planks.
  • Go easy with the foam roller: the IT band is extremely tough and does not lengthen no matter how hard you roll it. If you roll, gently loosen the tensor fasciae latae and hip muscles above it rather than the band itself.
  • When you run again: once walking and stairs are pain-free, start short and slow on flat ground. Leave downhills for last, since they load the IT band heavily.

See a doctor if

  • the knee swells, feels warm or feels full of fluid
  • the knee catches, gives way or will not straighten fully (to check for a meniscus injury)
  • the pain has not eased after one to two weeks
  • it hurts at rest or at night, or pressing on the bone causes sharp pain (to check for a stress fracture)

For the next full marathon

In my next full marathon, I want to test my self-control by holding back the excitement of the first 15 km to 5'05" to 5'10" per km. As an orthopedic surgeon, I will add one more thing: ten minutes of hip abductor exercises after my morning runs. The legs that carry me past 35 km and the hips that protect the outside of my knee the next day are built in the same place.

Believing that three minutes saved in the first half can protect ten minutes after 35 km, I tie my running shoes again in the morning.
