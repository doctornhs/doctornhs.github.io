A painful bump on the inside of the foot: accessory navicular and the Kidner procedure

Hee Seung Nam, MD, Orthopedic Surgeon · October 21, 2026

This patient had pain on the inner side of the left foot for a long time and had already been advised to have surgery at another hospital. Pressing the bump on the inner side of the foot, just in front of and below the ankle bone, was painful. The X-ray showed an accessory navicular.

What is an accessory navicular?

The navicular is a boat-shaped bone at the top of the arch on the inner side of the foot. The posterior tibial tendon, which runs down the inside of the calf, attaches to it and pulls the arch up. Some people have an extra small bone on the inner side of the navicular. This is the accessory navicular. It is a piece that should have fused into one bone during growth but stayed separate, and it is common, found in roughly one in ten people.

It is classified into three types by how it joins the navicular.

TypeFeatures
Type 1A small round bone inside the tendon, separate from the navicular. Usually painless
Type 2Joined to the navicular by cartilage. This junction is pulled and injured repeatedly, so it is the most often painful
Type 3Fused to the navicular by bone, making a horn-like bump. Shoe pressure can make it hurt

Why does it hurt?

  • The tendon pulls on it. Much of the posterior tibial tendon attaches to the accessory navicular, so the junction between the two bones is pulled with every step and stride.
  • The junction gets injured. Repeated pulling or an ankle sprain causes small injuries and inflammation at the junction.
  • Shoes press on it. The bump rubs against the inside of the shoe, and the skin and tissue beneath swell and hurt.

So the typical symptom is pain and swelling on the inner side of the foot after long walks or exercise, or in stiff shoes. It often comes with flat feet.

Treatment to try first

Having an accessory navicular does not by itself call for treatment. When it hurts, non-surgical treatment comes first.

  • Reduce activity and use ice. Anti-inflammatory pain relievers can help.
  • Wear roomy shoes that do not press on the inner side, with insoles that support the arch.
  • If the pain is severe, rest the foot for a short time in an ankle brace or cast.

If the pain persists or keeps coming back and gets in the way of daily life, surgery is considered. This patient had been in pain for a long time and had already been advised to have surgery elsewhere, so we decided to operate.

Surgery: removing the extra bone and reattaching the tendon

The surgery was done under spinal anesthesia and took less than an hour. It is called the Kidner procedure, after the surgeon who first described it.

  • Incision: I made a lengthwise incision along the bump on the inner side of the foot and exposed the accessory navicular and the posterior tibial tendon attached to it.
  • Removing the extra bone: The accessory navicular was carefully detached from the navicular and the tendon.
  • Preparing the bone bed: I lightly shaved the inner surface of the remaining navicular to make a bleeding bone bed, so the tendon can heal onto the bone.
  • Reattaching the tendon: The posterior tibial tendon was advanced and firmly reattached to the navicular with a pull-out suture. I checked that its tension and fixation were adequate.
  • Finishing: After washing and closing the wound in layers, a short leg splint was applied with the foot turned slightly inward and pointed down. This position keeps the tendon from being pulled while it heals.

There is also simple excision, which removes only the accessory navicular. Which method is better is still debated, but both relieve pain in most patients. The Kidner procedure, which reattaches the tendon, has the advantage of making the tendon's attachment secure.

Recovery after surgery

The tendon needs time to heal onto the bone, so recovery is not rushed. It varies with the method and the foot, but it usually goes like this.

TimeWhat happens
Surgery to 2 weeksSplint and elevation. No weight on the operated foot, using crutches. Wound check and stitch removal
2 to 6 weeksCast or brace. Weight-bearing begins gradually depending on progress
After 6 weeksOut of the cast and walking. Ankle and strengthening exercises. Arch-support insoles
Around 3 monthsGradual return to light running and sports

The inner side of the foot may be swollen or numb for a while after surgery. If the wound becomes red and swollen or starts to drain, see a doctor right away.

Final thoughts

  • An accessory navicular is a common variation of the bones, and having one does not mean it will hurt or need surgery.
  • If the bump on the inner side of the foot hurts, start with shoes, insoles and rest.
  • If it still hurts for a long time, surgery to remove the extra bone and reattach the tendon can relieve the pain.
  • After surgery, about six weeks of immobilization and protection while the tendon heals are important.

I hope the foot that hurt for so long becomes comfortable so the patient can walk freely again.

This article is general medical information. Treatment and recovery vary with the type of accessory navicular, the shape of the foot and the symptoms, so please confirm with your doctor.
