The cause of buttock pain hidden in a lumbar MRI: ankylosing spondylitis

Hee Seung Nam, MD, Orthopedic Surgeon · October 25, 2025

A 19-year-old man came in with pain in the right buttock and pelvis that had lasted three years. He rated the pain 10 out of 10, and said that on waking his body was so stiff he could not get up right away.

Another hospital had treated it as a back problem and taken a lumbar MRI, and he had been told there was nothing seriously wrong with his discs. But the real cause was hiding in that very MRI.

The clue in the lumbar MRI

On the sagittal view (from the side) the spine looks nearly normal. But a close look at the coronal view (from the front) shows inflammation in the sacroiliac joints, where the sacrum meets the pelvic bones. A lumbar MRI is usually taken to look at the discs, so the sacroiliac joints at the bottom edge of the image are easy to miss.

Confirming it with pelvic X-ray and MRI

The pelvic X-ray showed whitened, hardened bone around both sacroiliac joints (sclerosis) and small pits in the joint surfaces (erosions), findings that suggest early sacroiliitis. So we took a new pelvic MRI.

This time the bilateral sacroiliitis was even clearer. On examination, the Patrick test, in which the leg is placed in a figure-four position and pressed, caused pain on the right. Strongly suspecting ankylosing spondylitis, we tested for the HLA-B27 gene, and the result was positive.

What is ankylosing spondylitis?

Ankylosing spondylitis is an immune disease that causes repeated inflammation of the spine and sacroiliac joints. The immune system misfires and attacks the joints and the places where tendons attach to bone. When inflammation recurs for a long time, the vertebrae can fuse with bone and stiffen, called ankylosis, which gives the disease its name.

  • It usually starts between the late teens and the 30s and is more common in men.
  • It is closely linked to the HLA-B27 gene. In a study of 160 Korean patients with ankylosing spondylitis, 96.3% were HLA-B27 positive.
  • On the other hand, about 4–6% of the general population also carry this gene, and most of them never develop the disease. So a positive HLA-B27 alone does not make the diagnosis; symptoms and imaging are considered together.

How is it different from ordinary back pain?

The pain of ankylosing spondylitis differs from common muscular or disc-related back pain. The Assessment of SpondyloArthritis international Society (ASAS) considers back pain inflammatory back pain when at least four of the five features below are present.

Starts before age 40Any age
Gradual onsetOften starts suddenly after strain
Improves with movementWorsens with movement
Does not improve with restImproves with rest

Other features can include morning stiffness lasting more than 30 minutes, buttock pain that alternates between sides, knee or heel pain, and uveitis, a red and painful eye.

Why is diagnosis often late?

Back and buttock pain is common in young people, and early on X-rays often show no change. In a meta-analysis of 64 studies, it took an average of 6.7 years from the start of symptoms to diagnosis. This patient also went three years without knowing the cause.

Even before bone changes appear on X-ray, MRI can show inflammation in the bone around the sacroiliac joints (bone marrow edema). If a young person has back or buttock pain lasting more than three months that matches the features above, it is worth checking the sacroiliac joints too.

Treatment and management

Ankylosing spondylitis is a condition you live with by controlling the inflammation rather than curing it. It is often treated together with rheumatology, and the main principles of the 2022 international (ASAS-EULAR) recommendations are as follows.

  • Exercise and education: regular stretching and exercise are the foundation of treatment. Swimming, walking and exercises that extend the spine are good, with physical therapy if needed.
  • Quitting smoking: smoking is linked to disease progression, so quitting is important.
  • Anti-inflammatory painkillers (NSAIDs): the first-line medicine for pain and stiffness. If they work well and symptoms persist, they may be taken continuously.
  • Biologics: if NSAIDs do not control the disease, injectable drugs such as TNF inhibitors or IL-17 inhibitors are considered.
  • Steroids: long-term systemic steroids are not recommended for spinal symptoms.

In closing

  • Back or buttock pain lasting more than three months at a young age, especially pain that is stiff in the morning and eases with movement, can point to ankylosing spondylitis.
  • Even if you have been told your lumbar MRI is normal, the sacroiliac joints may need a separate look.
  • HLA-B27 only helps the diagnosis; a positive result does not mean everyone has the disease.
  • With early diagnosis, and inflammation controlled through exercise and medication, everyday life can be maintained well.
