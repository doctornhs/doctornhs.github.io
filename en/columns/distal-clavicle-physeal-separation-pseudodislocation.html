A child's AC joint dislocation may be a 'pseudodislocation'

Hee Seung Nam, MD, Orthopedic Surgeon · November 24, 2026

Imagine a child who falls and hurts a shoulder, and on the X-ray the outer end of the collarbone sits higher than the top of the shoulder blade (the acromion), with the gap between the collarbone and the coracoid process below it (the coracoclavicular distance) wider than on the other side. In an adult, this is clearly an AC joint dislocation, and if the gap is large, surgery with a hook plate may be considered.

In a child, the story is different. Studying this injury again recently reminded me that the formulas we use for adults should not be applied to children as they are. Here is what I learned, with illustrations I drew myself.

A child's clavicle ends differently from an adult's

  • An end you cannot see on X-ray: the outer end of a child's clavicle is a small piece of cartilage (the epiphysis). Cartilage does not show on X-ray, so the clavicle looks shorter than it is. Bone usually appears in this epiphysis around age 19 to 20 and fuses to the shaft within a few months.
  • A thick periosteal sleeve: a child's periosteum is thick and tough and wraps the clavicle like a sleeve. It is firmly attached, together with the AC joint ligaments, to the cartilage end.
  • The ligaments hold the sleeve: the coracoclavicular ligaments that tether the clavicle downward attach to this periosteal sleeve rather than to bone.
  • The weak link is the growth plate: in children the growth plate is weaker than the ligaments. With the same force, an adult tears ligaments, while a child splits the bone at the growth plate.

How a pseudodislocation happens

When a child falls onto the outside of the shoulder, the clavicle shaft separates at the growth plate and escapes upward (sometimes backward) through a tear in the periosteal sleeve. The cartilage end stays attached at the AC joint, and the coracoclavicular ligaments stay attached to the sleeve.

Think of a crab stick: the shell (the periosteal sleeve) stays put, and only the filling (the clavicle shaft) slips out.

Because the cartilage end does not show on X-ray, it looks exactly like an AC joint dislocation, with the clavicle separated from the acromion and raised. That is why this injury is called a pseudodislocation. In a true AC dislocation the joint and ligaments are torn; in a pseudodislocation the joint is intact and the bone has split at the growth plate. It is the childhood counterpart of the adult AC separation. I have written a separate article on adult AC dislocation and its surgery.

Does a wide coracoclavicular distance mean the ligaments are torn?

This was the question that confused me most while studying. In an adult, a coracoclavicular distance more than twice normal means the ligaments are torn. In a pseudodislocation, however, the ligaments stay in place with the periosteal sleeve, and the gap looks wide only because the clavicle shaft has left the sleeve and risen.

One study reviewed 58 children aged 5 to 16 with distal clavicle injuries over 15 years. Most had a wide coracoclavicular distance on X-ray that suggested AC separation, but a distal clavicle fracture was found in 45, and long-term results with nonoperative treatment were excellent. The authors warned that AC separation in children is overdiagnosed and overtreated. As early as the early 1980s, it had been argued that true AC dislocations are rare in children.

In severe cases, though, MRI can show the coracoclavicular ligaments pulled off the periosteum. A wide gap does not prove the ligaments are intact either. So when the X-ray leaves doubt, an MRI is the way to check.

What MRI shows

  • Whether the cartilage end sits in place beside the acromion, and where the shaft separated from it
  • Where and how badly the periosteal sleeve is torn
  • Whether the escaped clavicle has pierced the trapezius muscle behind it
  • Whether the coracoclavicular ligaments are still attached to the periosteum or pulled off

In adults, stress X-rays with weights hanging from the wrists can show instability, but injured children tolerate them poorly. MRI shows the cartilage, periosteum and ligaments directly and helps with surgical planning.

The shell left behind builds a new clavicle

The most remarkable thing about this injury is the power of the sleeve left behind. A child's periosteum is very good at making bone, so it fills the empty shell with new bone. That is why most of these injuries heal well with just a sling.

That power cuts both ways. If the escaped end is left badly displaced, the new bone made by the sleeve and the raised old bone can harden separately, leaving a thick or double distal clavicle. This raises a fair question: if a deformity could remain, wouldn't surgery from the start be better?

So when is surgery needed?

The answer depends on how far and in which direction the clavicle has moved, and on the child's age. These injuries are graded into six types by how badly the periosteal sleeve is torn and where the clavicle has gone.

TypeInjuryUsual treatment
IAC ligament sprain, periosteal sleeve intactMostly nonoperative with a sling
IIPartial tear of the periosteal sleeve

VIClavicle displaced below the coracoid

A 2023 systematic review of AC joint injuries in adolescents lists these as indications for surgery.

  • An open fracture, with bone through the skin
  • Displacement greater than the width of the clavicle
  • A bone end pressing on the skin and about to break through
  • The clavicle piercing the trapezius
  • Pressure on nerves or blood vessels
  • Severe shortening of the shoulder girdle

If none of these apply, MRI is used to judge the extent of injury, and minimally displaced injuries are treated without surgery. Teenagers near the end of growth have less remodeling ability, so the balance may tip a little further toward surgery.

When surgery is done, the escaped clavicle is put back inside the periosteal sleeve and the torn periosteum is repaired. Fixation in the studies most often used K-wires (thin pins), an absorbable tension band, or nonabsorbable sutures. The hook plate, commonly used for adult AC dislocation, was used in that review mainly for true AC dislocations in adolescents, and evidence for its use in children with open growth plates is still limited. The review itself notes that the evidence is low-level and varies widely between studies, so no single answer can be drawn.

Final thoughts

  • Even when a child's shoulder X-ray looks like an AC joint dislocation, it is often a pseudodislocation, with a split at the growth plate of the clavicle.
  • The coracoclavicular ligaments often stay attached to the periosteal sleeve, so a wide gap does not by itself mean they are torn.
  • Because the sleeve left behind makes new bone, most of these injuries heal well with a sling.
  • If the clavicle is badly displaced, has gone backward through the muscle, threatens the skin, or the teen is near the end of growth, check with MRI and consider surgery.

The biggest lesson from studying this was a simple one. A child's shoulder is not a small adult shoulder. Before applying an adult formula to a single X-ray, think first of the cartilage end that the picture does not show and the periosteal sleeve that stays in place.
