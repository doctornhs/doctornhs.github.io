An arm hit against a wall after a slip: a long, twisted humeral shaft fracture

Hee Seung Nam, MD, Orthopedic Surgeon · November 12, 2026

A patient came to the emergency room after slipping and hitting her left arm hard against a wall. She had not lost consciousness, but her left upper arm was very painful and she could not move it.

X-ray: a long, twisted break in the upper arm bone

The body, or shaft, of the upper arm bone (humerus) had broken in a long twist: a spiral fracture. This pattern is common when the arm takes a twisting force in a fall.

Wait with a brace, or operate?

Unlike other long bones, humeral shaft fractures often heal well without surgery. The shoulder and elbow move through a wide range, so a slight bend or shortening of the bone rarely affects function. For this reason, a functional brace that wraps the upper arm while the bone heals has long been used.

But bracing has drawbacks too. In a Finnish randomized trial comparing bracing with plate fixation, arm function scores at one year were similar, but a considerable number of braced patients ended up having surgery because the bone did not heal. Wearing a brace for months is also inconvenient.

TreatmentProsCons
Functional braceNo surgery or anesthesia. Heals well in many casesBrace worn for months. Some do not heal and end up needing surgery
Plate fixationBone aligned and fixed firmly right away. The arm can be moved earlyRisks of wound problems, infection and nerve injury

In this patient the fracture line was long and badly displaced, and she wanted to use her arm soon, so we decided to fix it surgically three days after the injury.

Surgery: fixing with a plate while protecting the radial nerve

The structure to be most careful about in humeral surgery is the radial nerve. It spirals around the back of the upper arm bone and drives the muscles that straighten the wrist and fingers. If injured, the wrist can droop: wrist drop. About 12% of humeral shaft fractures are reported to come with radial nerve palsy from the injury itself, so the ability to extend the wrist and fingers is always checked before surgery. Fortunately, this patient had no nerve palsy.

Through a long incision running from the front of the shoulder down the outer side of the upper arm, the radial nerve was found first at the lower end of the wound and protected throughout. Blood clot and tissue trapped in the fracture were cleared, the spiral fragments were aligned and temporarily held with a wire. A long locking plate was then placed on the outer side of the humerus and fixed with screws into the head above and the shaft below. Intraoperative fluoroscopy confirmed good alignment and appropriate screw lengths.

After surgery: the bone healing

After surgery, the arm was supported comfortably in a sling, and shoulder and elbow exercises began early as the pain eased. Follow-up X-rays showed new bone forming along the fracture as it healed, and she is now doing well.

In closing

  • Humeral shaft fractures often heal with a brace, but surgery is considered when displacement is large, when the arm needs to be used soon, or when the bone does not heal.
  • The radial nerve that winds around the upper arm bone can be injured at the time of injury or during surgery, so the ability to extend the wrist and fingers must be checked.
  • If your wrist droops or the back of your hand goes numb after an injury, tell your doctor right away.
  • Whatever the treatment, steady rehabilitation to keep the shoulder and elbow from stiffening is important.

This article is general medical information. Treatment can differ with the fracture pattern and the patient's condition, so please confirm through a medical consultation.
