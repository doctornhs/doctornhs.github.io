The Nobel Prize inside a joint injection: how many “bone shots” are too many?

Hee Seung Nam, MD, Orthopedic Surgeon · November 14, 2026

This column is adapted from “One Joint Injection,” a chapter of my forthcoming book The Nobel Prize That Arrived in the Clinic (in Korean).

A 58-year-old woman had not slept properly for months. Lying on her right shoulder woke her with a jolt of pain; she could not reach her back pocket, and she tied her hair with her left hand only. She could not lift the arm to shoulder height, and neither could I when I lifted it for her. The capsule around the joint had thickened and contracted with inflammation: adhesive capsulitis, better known as frozen shoulder.

Under ultrasound guidance I passed a needle into the joint from the front and watched the medication spread on the screen. Two weeks later she walked into the clinic and said:

“Doctor, last night I slept through without waking for the first time.”

It is one of the best things an orthopedic surgeon gets to hear.

That same afternoon, a 75-year-old woman came in tapping her knee. “Just give me that shot again. Last time it kept me comfortable for a month.” Her chart showed it would be her fifth this year, more if you counted the ones from her previous clinic. Patients in Korea call it the “bone shot.” One rumor says it works like a miracle; another says too many will melt your bones. Both are half true.

I suggested we try something other than an injection this time, and was met with a disappointed look. This column is about the Nobel Prize that sits between those two scenes.

1948: a 29-year-old gets out of bed

In 1929, Philip Hench, a rheumatologist at the Mayo Clinic, noticed something odd. A patient with long-standing rheumatoid arthritis developed jaundice, and her joint pain almost vanished; when the jaundice cleared, the arthritis returned. Hench also knew that pregnant patients with rheumatoid arthritis often improved during pregnancy. Perhaps something common to jaundice and pregnancy was putting out the fire in the joints. He called it “substance X.”

At the same clinic, the biochemist Edward Kendall was grinding up tons of cattle adrenal glands to isolate their hormones, while Tadeus Reichstein in Switzerland was working out the structures of the same compounds. The problem was quantity. Only after Lewis Sarett, a chemist at Merck, synthesized “compound E” from cattle bile in dozens of steps was there enough to give to a patient.

On September 21, 1948, a 29-year-old woman who could barely get out of bed because of rheumatoid arthritis received an injection of compound E. Within three days she was up, and a week later, the story goes, she went shopping downtown for three hours. When Hench showed films of his patients before and after treatment the following year, the audience of physicians rose to applaud. Compound E was named cortisone, and the three men shared the 1950 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, just two years after the first patient was treated.

From the whole body to a single joint

The miracle came in large doses. Early patients received hundreds of milligrams a day for months, and the bill soon arrived: rounded, puffy faces, fragile bones that broke, infections, rising blood sugar and blood pressure. Within a few years cortisone had gone from miracle drug to feared drug.

That is when the orthopedic joint injection was born. In 1951, Joseph Hollander in Philadelphia stopped flooding the whole body and put the drug into the one painful joint. Systemic side effects fell, and the fire in the joint went out. The injection I give into a frozen shoulder today is Hollander’s injection.

Orthopedic surgeons also see the shadow of this prize more than most. In people who take steroids for a long time, the head of the thigh bone can lose its blood supply and die (avascular necrosis of the femoral head), and the spine can collapse after a minor fall. Through one door comes cortisone’s gift; through the other comes its bill.

How many “bone shots” are too many?

One trial gave patients with knee osteoarthritis either a steroid or saline injection every three months for two years. The steroid group had no better pain relief, and slightly more cartilage loss. There are also reports of joints breaking down faster than expected after injections, and studies suggesting that an injection within three months before a joint replacement may raise the risk of infection after surgery.

A common rule of thumb is no more than three or four injections a year into the same joint, at least three months apart. More important than the count, though, is deciding first whether this pain is something an injection can tide over, or something whose cause needs to be treated. An injection puts out the fire; it does not fix why the fire started.

Worth asking before an injection

  • What is causing the pain, and will the injection help that cause?
  • When was the last injection into this joint, including at other clinics?
  • What else can be done alongside it: strengthening exercise, weight management, oral medication, daily habits
  • If surgery such as a joint replacement is planned, how the timing fits with the injection

Closing: knowing when to stop

In my journal there is a page where I wrote down the three things a treatment can do, as the Korean monk Ven. Pomnyun once put it: relieve pain, stop the disease from progressing, or slow its progress. A joint injection does only the first. Sometimes that is enough to give a frozen shoulder back its nights; sometimes it is not enough to protect a knee ten years from now.

The hard part is not the knowledge but telling those two apart. The patient wants relief now, the injection is quick and usually works, and it earns the clinic money. When all three point the same way, only the doctor can say, “Let’s not do it this time.” Since the day Hench’s patient got out of bed, the biggest question cortisone has left us is not what it can do, but when to stop.
