How should a child's clavicle fracture be treated?

Hee Seung Nam, MD, Orthopedic Surgeon · November 5, 2026

A child's clavicle fracture is different from an adult's

Clavicle fractures in children and adolescents have very distinctive features compared with adults. The biggest are an excellent ability to form bone and a thick periosteum, the membrane covering the bone. A child's periosteum is much thicker and tougher than an adult's, so even when the bone breaks it acts as a hinge (periosteal hinge) that keeps the broken ends from tearing badly through the surrounding tissue.

Thanks to this, good results can be expected from treatment without surgery even when a child's clavicle fracture is somewhat displaced. Rather than forcing an open operation, the ideal approach is to protect the environment around the bone so the child's own healing ability can work to the full.

A 12-year-old's clavicle fracture

A 12-year-old boy seen recently shows this well. He came in with a left clavicle fracture, and front and cephalic tilt (cephalic oblique) X-rays clearly showed the outer hard layer (cortex) broken and displaced. It was not a case that absolutely required surgery, such as bone pressing on the skin or an open fracture, but I judged that treatment to reduce the displacement was needed.

Aligning without an incision: closed reduction

In the operating room, while watching on a real-time fluoroscope (C-arm), the bone was aligned by hand without opening the skin: a closed reduction.

Comparing before and after, the displaced ends found their place and the cortex became smoothly continuous. It is hard to call it a 100% perfect reduction like fixation with metal, but aligning it without an incision is very meaningful. Opening the skin to expose the fracture damages the tiny blood vessels around the bone and washes away the fracture hematoma, which is rich in bone-forming factors and stem cells. A reduction without an incision keeps this natural healing environment intact.

From one week to three months: how the bone heals

Worried that the bone might slip again if X-rays were taken right after reduction, we took the next X-ray one week later. Fortunately, the cortex remained continuous on both the front and tilt views.

Around 3 to 4 weeks, as the bone heals, callus forms at the fracture and may be felt as a lump under the skin. This shows the bone is healing well, and as the child grows the bone is reshaped (remodeling) and gradually flattens over months to years, so there is no need to worry. To prevent further displacement and help stable healing, this boy kept a figure-of-eight bandage for three weeks starting one week after the reduction.

In closing

  • A child's clavicle has a thick periosteum and strong bone-forming ability, so most heal well without surgery even when somewhat displaced.
  • When displacement is large, a closed reduction without an incision can correct the position while protecting the healing environment.
  • The lump (callus) felt during healing is normal and mostly flattens as the child grows.
  • Surgery is considered, however, when bone has broken or threatens to break through the skin, when nerve or blood vessel injury is suspected, or in late adolescents with severe displacement and shortening.

I believe conservative treatment that makes the most of a child's natural healing power is the safest and best choice for long-term results in young patients.

This article is general medical information. Treatment can differ with the fracture pattern and the child's age and condition, so please confirm through a medical consultation.
