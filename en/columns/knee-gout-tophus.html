Gouty tophus of the knee

Hee Seung Nam, MD, Orthopedic Surgeon · November 4, 2026

A 56-year-old man came in with a lump on the front of his left knee. He already had gout, and he said the lump had appeared three years earlier and kept growing. The front of the kneecap was red and swollen, and it hurt when pressed. A small wound had even formed in the middle of the thinned skin, so the lump was about to burst out.

MRI: a mass covering the front of the kneecap

The MRI he brought from another hospital showed a large mass under the skin in front of the kneecap (patella). Its inside was uneven, like several small masses clustered together. When a lump like this appears around a joint in someone with gout, a tophus is the first thing to suspect. Still, what a lump really is becomes certain only after it is removed and examined under the microscope.

Surgery: a mass that looked as if chalk dust had been ground into it

The lump was about to break through the skin and it was painful, so we decided to remove it surgically. The skin over a tophus is thin and has poor blood supply, so the wound can be slow to heal after surgery; I explained this to him before the operation.

It was a tophus. The first thing that catches the eye is the chalk-white or pale yellow material embedded throughout the tissue. These are urate crystals, formed as uric acid that had circulated in the blood for years built up and hardened. This look, as if dry chalk dust or plaster had been ground in, is the most distinctive feature of a tophus.

The red, irregular tissue surrounding the white masses is chronic inflammatory tissue, formed as the body treats the urate crystals as foreign material and attacks them without end. As urate crystals grow they can burrow into nearby ligaments, the joint capsule and even bone, so in the photo the edges are not smooth but tangled with the surrounding tissue. A tophus this large means the problem has gone beyond pain: the urate mass has begun to damage the tissue around it.

Pathology showed fine needle-shaped crystals surrounded by granulomatous inflammation and multinucleated giant cells, confirming a tophus. Because the lump was red and swollen we were also worried about infection, but the bacterial culture sent at the same time grew no bacteria.

What is a tophus?

Gout is a disease in which uric acid stays high in the blood for a long time and forms needle-shaped crystals that build up in joints and the tissues around them. When the crystals trigger inflammation, a joint suddenly swells and becomes very painful: a gout attack. Left untreated for a long time, the crystals clump into a mass, called a tophus.

  • Tophi often form at the big toe, top of the foot, ankle, Achilles tendon, back of the elbow, fingers and the rim of the ear, and, as in this patient, they can also form in front of the knee.
  • They usually grow slowly without pain, but when inflamed they become red, swollen and painful.
  • As they grow, the skin thins and can burst, letting white chalky material leak out, and bacteria can enter the wound and cause infection.
  • Gout is steadily increasing in Korea. A study of national health insurance data found that prevalence more than doubled, from 3.5 per 1,000 people in 2007 to 7.6 per 1,000 in 2015, and it was more common in men.

Lumps in front of the knee

The front of the kneecap has a bursa (a small fluid sac) just under the skin, so lumps often form there. They are often hard to tell apart by appearance alone, so the history, examination and ultrasound or MRI are considered together.

As in this patient, an inflamed tophus becomes red and swollen and can look like a bacterial infection. That is why, at surgery, the removed tissue is sent for both pathology and bacterial culture.

When a tophus should be removed

Most tophi slowly shrink when uric acid is lowered with medication. Surgery is considered in cases like these:

  • When the skin has thinned and is about to break or has already broken, or when infection has set in
  • When the tophus gets in the way of joint movement or wearing shoes, or presses on a nerve
  • When the pain is severe
  • When it needs to be confirmed whether the lump is a tophus or something else

Wound care is central to tophus surgery. The skin over a tophus is thin and poorly supplied with blood, so wounds tend to heal slowly or reopen, and infection or other medical conditions increase complications. In a Taiwanese study of 303 tophus operations, leg wounds took an average of 19 days to heal, longer than arm wounds (13 days), and leg wounds with infection before surgery took even longer, 31 days. This is why operating before the skin breaks and becomes infected favors recovery.

Surgery is a beginning, not the end

Surgery removed the large tophus pressing on the knee, but that does not end the treatment. A tophus may look like a tumor, but it is not one; it is a mass caused by a problem with uric acid metabolism. Removing the visible mass leaves the uric acid stored throughout the body untouched, so if blood uric acid stays high, new tophi can form at other joints.

  • Take urate-lowering medication consistently. The American College of Rheumatology strongly recommends starting urate-lowering therapy if there is even one tophus under the skin. Allopurinol is the first-line drug, started at a low dose and increased gradually.
  • Keep to the target uric acid level. The basic goal is to keep serum uric acid below 6 mg/dL. For severe gout with tophi, EULAR (the European rheumatology alliance) recommends lowering it further, below 5 mg/dL, until the crystals dissolve.
  • The lower the uric acid, the faster tophi shrink. In a study following 63 patients with tophi, the lower the serum uric acid during treatment, the faster the tophi shrank. The small crystals left in the tissue also need to be dissolved slowly this way for the surgical site to heal well and to prevent recurrence.
  • Attacks can actually occur in the first months of treatment. This happens as stored crystals dissolve and are released, so for the first 3 to 6 months a preventive drug such as colchicine is taken as well. It is important not to stop urate-lowering medication because of an attack without talking to your doctor.
  • Change daily habits too. Cut down on alcohol (especially beer), sugary drinks high in fructose, organ meats and red meat, and manage your weight. Diet alone, however, can hardly dissolve tophi that have already formed, so medication is the mainstay.

In closing

  • Gout is easy to dismiss as a disease that hurts for a while and then goes away. But if early pain is ignored, uric acid can clump into masses that try to break through the skin, as in this case, or damage joints and bone.
  • If you feel an unexplained lump around your knee or toes, or the same spot swells and hurts again and again, see a specialist and get checked.
  • If the skin over a lump turns red and thin, or white material shows through, it is best to be seen before it bursts.
  • Surgery relieves pressure and pain right away and improves quality of life, but controlling gout properly requires steady medication together with dietary changes.

After suffering for so long, I hope he can now return to daily life with a lighter step.

This article is general medical information. Diagnosis and treatment can differ with the location and size of the lump, uric acid levels and other medical conditions, so please confirm through a medical consultation.
