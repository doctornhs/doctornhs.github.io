PRP vs hyaluronic acid: what do the meta-analyses say?

Hee Seung Nam, MD, Orthopedic Surgeon · November 21, 2026

“I heard PRP is better than the cartilage shot. Is it?” I hear this often in clinic these days. Online you will find “a meta-analysis shows PRP beats hyaluronic acid,” and right beside it “a study shows PRP doesn’t work.” Both statements come from real papers. What differs is what the injection was compared with.

In this column I go through what the meta-analyses actually say. Steroid injections (the “bone shot”), with their intervals and effect on blood sugar, are covered separately in “Knee injections: how often is safe?”

The short answer first

  • Meta-analyses that compare PRP and hyaluronic acid head to head generally report better pain and function scores with PRP at 6 to 12 months.
  • But large studies that compare each of them with saline (a placebo injection) found small or no differences.
  • There is no evidence that either injection rebuilds worn cartilage.
  • Guidelines disagree. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons says PRP may be considered on limited evidence and advises against routine hyaluronic acid; the American College of Rheumatology recommends against both.
  • In Korea, hyaluronic acid is covered by national health insurance, while knee PRP was recognized as a new health technology in late 2024 but is not covered.

How are the two injections different?

Hyaluronic acidReplaces the slippery substance that is naturally present in joint fluid, to lubricate and cushion the joint. In Korea it is often called the “cartilage shot,” but it does not make cartilage.
PRP (platelet-rich plasma)Your own blood is drawn and spun in a centrifuge to concentrate the platelets. The idea is that growth factors from platelets calm inflammation and help tissue recover.

Both are injected into the joint space.

Hyaluronic acid is commonly given as three injections a week apart, and single-injection products also exist.1 PRP is made from 20 to 30 mL of blood, and the catch is that the method differs between clinics and devices. Platelet concentration, whether white blood cells are included and the number of injections vary from study to study, so “PRP” is really several different injections under one name.

Head-to-head meta-analyses: PRP came out ahead

A meta-analysis published in 2021 in the American Journal of Sports Medicine pooled 18 randomized controlled trials, comparing 811 patients given PRP with 797 given hyaluronic acid over an average of 11 months. The WOMAC total score (a knee arthritis pain and function score) improved by an average of 44.7% with PRP and 12.6% with hyaluronic acid. Of the 11 trials that reported pain scores (VAS), 6 found PRP significantly better, and PRP with the white blood cells filtered out did better than PRP rich in white blood cells.2

Another meta-analysis from 2020, pooling 20 randomized trials, also reported that PRP reduced pain more than hyaluronic acid at 6 and 12 months.3 Up to this point, the answer looks simple.

But compared with a placebo injection

There is one question to ask of any meta-analysis: what was it compared with? Hyaluronic acid is often the comparison in PRP trials, so first we need to know how much hyaluronic acid itself does.

Hyaluronic acid. A 2022 meta-analysis in the BMJ pooled 169 trials with 21,163 people. Looking only at the 24 large, high-quality trials (8,997 people), hyaluronic acid reduced pain very slightly more than placebo, but the difference was below the smallest change patients can notice. In 15 large trials (6,462 people) the risk of serious adverse events was 1.49 times that of placebo, and there were signs of publication bias, with smaller studies showing larger effects.4

PRP. The RESTORE trial, published in JAMA in 2021, gave PRP or saline to 288 people aged 50 or older with mild to moderate knee osteoarthritis and followed them for 12 months. Pain fell by 2.1 points out of 10 with PRP and 1.8 points with saline, with no meaningful difference, and cartilage volume on MRI decreased similarly in both groups (1.4% versus 1.2%).5

Taken together, these studies show something important. Simply having an injection has a large effect: even saline lowered pain scores by almost 2 points. So in trials with a weak comparison or loose blinding, a new injection easily looks like the winner. “PRP beats hyaluronic acid” may be true, but it has to be read alongside the fact that hyaluronic acid itself does little.

The Korean assessment is somewhat more positive. A health technology reassessment by the National Evidence-based Healthcare Collaborating Agency (NECA) found that hyaluronic acid showed effects lasting three months or more, and that PRP may reduce pain and improve function, while noting as a limitation that PRP injection methods vary greatly and results differ widely between studies.6

The US guidelines are cautious about both injections because the evidence is weak or the effect small,7,8 while in Korea PRP was recognized as a safe and effective technology for relieving pain and improving function in KL grade 2–3 knee osteoarthritis that has not responded to existing treatment such as medication.9 The same evidence, read by different bodies, leads to conclusions of different temperatures.

So how should you choose?

Set your expectations first. Neither injection rebuilds cartilage. The goal is to lower pain enough to buy time for exercise, and the American College of Rheumatology guideline lists exercise and self-management programs as first-line treatment.8

Know your stage of arthritis. If the cartilage is almost completely worn away (KL grade 4), expectations for either injection are low, and Korean criteria cover hyaluronic acid for KL grades 1 to 3 and PRP for grades 2 to 3. At that stage it is time to discuss other options rather than switching injections.

Put cost and expected benefit side by side. PRP is not covered by insurance and is usually given more than once. Whether to spend that money for the chance of doing a little better than hyaluronic acid is your decision, but it is worth knowing from the numbers above how big that “little better” is.

Check private insurance in advance. In March 2024, Korea’s Financial Supervisory Service issued a consumer alert warning that people who receive expensive new-technology treatments on the assurance that private medical insurance will pay may be disappointed.10 Ask your insurer directly before treatment.

If PRP has been suggested, ask

  • What stage (KL grade) my arthritis is on X-ray
  • How many injections, and the total cost
  • How it is prepared (for example, whether white blood cells are filtered out)
  • How the effect will be judged afterwards, and what the next step is if it does not work
  • Whether private insurance covers it (check with the insurer yourself)

Whichever injection you choose, write down your current pain level and how far you can walk without stopping before the injection, and compare them three months later. That record helps the next decision more than anything else.

In closing

“PRP is better than hyaluronic acid” is only half true. PRP wins head to head, but hyaluronic acid does little to begin with, and PRP did not show a clear difference in a rigorous comparison with placebo.

I see this less as a question of which injection is stronger and more as a question of what to expect from an injection at all. An injection is a tool to lower pain so that you can start exercising; what keeps a knee going for the long run is thigh strength and weight control.
