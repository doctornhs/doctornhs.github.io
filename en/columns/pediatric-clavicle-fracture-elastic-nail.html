A 12-year-old's collarbone broken playing soccer: why I chose an elastic nail over a plate

Hee Seung Nam, MD, Orthopedic Surgeon · November 14, 2026

A 12-year-old boy tripped over another player's foot during a soccer game and fell onto his left shoulder. The X-ray in the emergency room showed that the middle of his left clavicle (collarbone) was completely broken and displaced.

In an earlier column, I described a child of the same age whose clavicle fracture was aligned by hand without an incision and healed in a bandage. Most children's clavicle fractures heal well without surgery. This column is about the other side: when surgery is chosen, what to fix the bone with, and how.

Why a broken clavicle shifts like this

The clavicle is an S-shaped strut that links the breastbone (sternum) to the shoulder. When it breaks in the middle, a neck muscle (the sternocleidomastoid) pulls the inner fragment up, while the weight of the arm drags the outer fragment down. This boy's X-ray shows the typical picture. A small fragment (a butterfly fragment) had also broken off between the two ends.

So we also took a CT scan, to see where the small fragment lay and how many pieces the fracture had.

Does a child's broken clavicle need surgery?

Usually not. In the FACTS study, eight children's hospitals in the United States followed 416 adolescents aged 10 to 18 with completely displaced clavicle fractures for two years. Shoulder function, quality of life and satisfaction did not differ between those who had surgery and those who did not. Those treated with plates actually had more unplanned reoperations (10.4% vs 1.4%) and more significant complications (20.8% vs 5.2%). Failure to heal was very rare, at 0.4%.

Surgery is still considered in some situations.

  • When surgery is needed: an open fracture with bone through the skin, a broken end pushing up on the skin from inside so that it may break through, nerve or blood vessel injury, or a fracture of the shoulder blade (scapula) at the same time
  • When it is decided together: marked displacement and shortening, an adolescent who has nearly finished growing, or a need to return to sport quickly

After discussing these points thoroughly with his parents, we decided to fix this boy's fracture surgically. The next question was what to fix it with.

Three options

MethodAdvantagesDrawbacks
SlingNo surgery or anesthesia. Most children heal wellPain for the first few weeks. A lump (callus) can be felt while it heals, and the bone may heal slightly short
PlateThe fracture is opened, the pieces are aligned and fixed firmlyA long incision and scar along the clavicle. The skin below the scar may feel numb, and removing the plate means opening the same area again
Elastic nailInserted inside the bone through a small incision near the inner end of the clavicle. Small scar, easy to removeLess stable in fractures with many pieces. The end of the nail can irritate the skin or back out

Why not a plate?

A plate is the most common operation for adult clavicle fractures and holds the bone firmly. In children, though, it comes at a cost. The skin along the clavicle must be opened widely and the membrane covering the bone (periosteum) stripped away. Small nerves that supply the skin of the upper chest (the supraclavicular nerves) cross the clavicle, so the skin below the scar can stay numb after surgery. The plate sits just under the skin where it can be felt, and because children usually have the plate removed after healing, the same area has to be opened a second time.

An elastic nail carries less of this burden. In a study comparing children aged 10 to 14, elastic nailing took about half the operating time (31 vs 60 minutes) and half the incision length (2.4 vs 5.4 cm) of plating, with no difference in shoulder function. Concern about the scar was much more common in the plate group.

What is an elastic nail?

An elastic nail is a thin, flexible titanium rod about 2 to 2.5 mm in diameter. Its tip is slightly bent, so when it is rotated as it is pushed in, it follows the curved channel inside the S-shaped clavicle (the medullary canal). The rod braces against the inside of the bone at several points, much like a splint placed inside the bone. Because the fracture is not opened, or only slightly, the periosteum and the blood clot at the fracture (fracture hematoma), both of which help the bone heal, are preserved.

This boy received a 2 mm nail. The diameter is chosen to match the width of the channel inside the clavicle rather than body weight. A nail that is too thin is wobbly; one that is too thick can jam in the channel or break through the bone wall.

The operation

  • Entry: A small incision is made a little outside the inner end of the clavicle (the breastbone end), and the nail enters through a hole in the bone. The clavicle's growth plate lies right next to the breastbone, so the entry is placed outside it.
  • Crossing the fracture: Under live X-ray (C-arm), the displaced bone is aligned and the nail is advanced across the fracture into the outer fragment. Large blood vessels and the lung lie just beneath the clavicle, so depth and direction are checked throughout.
  • The end: The inner end of the nail was left short, bent and buried under the skin. Left too long, it irritates the skin; cut too short, it is hard to grasp when it is removed later.

After surgery

  • A sling for 3 to 4 weeks: An elastic nail is a splint inside the bone and is not as rigid as a plate. For the first few weeks, a sling reduces the load on the clavicle. The authors of a study of 23 adolescents treated with elastic nails credited 3 to 4 weeks in a sling for the absence of nail bending or redisplacement.
  • Raising the arm, gradually: Gentle shoulder exercises start once the pain eases. For about six weeks, though, big movements that lift the arm above shoulder height are limited, because twisting forces on the clavicle can push the nail back out toward the inner end.
  • Return to sport: Sport is recommended after 4 weeks, and contact sports such as soccer after 8 weeks. In one study, the three children whose nails broke or bent were all injured during kickboxing, soccer or ice hockey. This matters especially for a boy who loves soccer.
  • Watching the nail end: Let us know right away if the skin over the inner clavicle turns red or something sharp can be felt. In the study of 23 adolescents, 2 (8.7%) had discomfort from the nail end, which resolved after the nail was removed.
  • Removing the nail: The nail is removed once X-rays show the bone is solidly healed. It is a simple procedure, reopening the entry point and pulling the nail out by its end, but children usually need a short anesthetic (see the column on hardware removal).

In closing

  • Even when completely displaced, most children's clavicle fractures heal well without surgery, and a large study found no difference in results compared with surgery.
  • Surgery is done when the skin, nerves or blood vessels are at risk; otherwise it is decided with the family, weighing age, displacement and sports plans.
  • If surgery is chosen, an elastic nail placed inside the bone through a small incision leaves a smaller scar and a simpler removal than a plate.
  • Because an elastic nail is a splint inside the bone, the sling and activity limits need to be followed so that it does not bend or back out.

This article is general medical information. Treatment can differ with age, fracture pattern and the condition of the skin, nerves and blood vessels, so please confirm through a medical consultation.
