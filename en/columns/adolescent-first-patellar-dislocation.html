A teenager's first kneecap dislocation: tighten it now, or reconstruct later?

Hee Seung Nam, MD, Orthopedic Surgeon · November 26, 2026

A girl in her mid-teens came in with a knee injury. Her kneecap (patella) had slipped out to the outside and popped back in: a first-time patellar dislocation. The MRI showed a torn MPFL (medial patellofemoral ligament), the ligament that holds the kneecap from the inner side.

Looking at the scans, I found myself weighing two paths. Should I tighten the stretched inner tissue now by folding and stitching it (plication)? Or would she be better off with a brace and exercises for now, and a ligament reconstruction once she has finished growing? Here is the latest evidence I found along the way, and how my thinking changed when I went back through the images.

What happens inside the knee when the kneecap dislocates

The kneecap glides up and down in a groove (the trochlea) at the end of the thighbone (femur). If you twist with the knee slightly bent, it can jump the groove and slip out to the outside. When that happens, the MPFL, which holds the kneecap from the inner side, tears in almost every case.

The kneecap usually pops back in on its own when the knee straightens. But as it does, the inner surface of the kneecap slams into the outer edge of the thighbone. Both spots get bone bruises, and with a harder impact a piece of cartilage or bone breaks off.

Path one: stitch it now

Folding and stitching the stretched inner tissue (plication), or reattaching the torn ligament where it came off (repair), is a relatively simple operation. It does not go near the growth plate, so it looks gentle on a child. But the long-term results were not what you might hope.

  • At two years it looked helpful. In Sweden, 74 children aged 9 to 14 with a first-time dislocation were randomly assigned to MPFL repair or a brace. Two years later, 22% of the repaired knees and 43% of the braced knees had dislocated again.
  • At ten years the difference was gone. Followed into adulthood, 62% of the repaired knees and 80% of the braced knees had dislocated again, a difference that was not statistically significant, and knee function was similar. The authors did not support routine repair after a first dislocation.
  • Against reconstruction, the gap was large. After folding (imbrication) of the inner tissue, 20.9% dislocated again, about four times the 5.1% after building a new ligament (reconstruction), and failures were more common in younger patients. In another study that followed patients with repeated dislocations for an average of 12 years, the rates were 41% after repair and 14% after reconstruction.

Stitching may seem to help at first, but it did not hold the kneecap through the long years in which a child grows and becomes more active. So I took plication off the table.

Path two: watch now, reconstruct after growth

What about holding off on surgery and reconstructing once she has stopped growing? That was not clearly better for her either.

  • There is little reason to wait. The main reason to delay reconstruction is the growth plate. But in a girl in her mid-teens the growth plate at the end of the thighbone is mostly closing, and on her MRI it remained only as a thin line. Even when the growth plate is open, there are ways to build the ligament while avoiding it; in a pooled analysis of such children, 3.8% dislocated again.
  • The kneecap can dislocate again while you wait. In 232 patients whose first dislocation happened before skeletal maturity, followed for an average of 12 years, the cumulative rate of re-dislocation was 21% at 2 years, 37% at 5 years and 54% at 15 years.
  • Damaged cartilage leads to arthritis. In the same study, knees with a cartilage or bone injury at the time of dislocation had a much higher risk of kneecap arthritis later on. In the ten-year study above, the children whose stabilizing surgery was delayed or never done after further instability had the poorest outcomes.

In the end, “wait until adulthood” was not a meaningful rule. If the kneecap dislocates again, reconstruct then; if the risk is high from the start, consider reconstruction from the start.

So a first dislocation is sorted by risk

Most first-time dislocations start with non-surgical care: a brace for a short time and exercises to strengthen the thigh and hip muscles. A Cochrane review pooling the trials still could not say for certain whether surgery lowers the chance of another dislocation. Instead, we try to pick out the children at high risk of it happening again. A widely used scheme in children and teenagers counts four risk factors:

  • Open growth plates: still growing
  • Trochlear dysplasia: a shallow or flat groove for the kneecap
  • Dislocation of the other knee: the other kneecap has also slipped out before
  • Patella alta: a high-riding kneecap (Caton-Deschamps index above 1.45)

Her growth plates were on the borderline of closing, and the shape of the groove was hard to judge from the images alone. On risk factors alone, things leaned toward non-surgical care.

Going back through the MRI changed my mind

Scrolling back through slice by slice, the outer edge of the thighbone (the lateral trochlear ridge) caught my eye. The band of cartilage, which should run smoothly, stopped short of the edge, and joint fluid lay directly against the bone. The bone underneath was bruised, and on the side view the cartilage at the same spot was lifted off the bone. This is exactly where the kneecap strikes as it snaps back in.

If cartilage has peeled off, the piece is somewhere inside the joint. Such a piece can wander, catch the knee and scrape the remaining cartilage. That is why surgery is usual when a piece of cartilage or bone has broken off, even after a first dislocation.

  • A large, healthy piece is fixed back in place. A piece of cartilage without bone was long thought unable to heal and was often removed. But in a pooled analysis of 76 young patients whose cartilage-only pieces were fixed, 96% healed, and the outer thighbone (lateral femoral condyle) was the most common site.
  • A small or broken-up piece is removed.
  • The MPFL is reconstructed, not stitched. Teenagers whose loose pieces were treated without reconstructing the MPFL had a 61% rate of further instability. In the same situation, reconstruction brought that down to 10%, against 58.7% after repair or no treatment of the ligament. In 40 knees that had the piece fixed and the MPFL reconstructed together, 95% were stable without a cartilage restoration procedure at an average of 2.6 years, though 28% needed another operation to tidy unhealed cartilage or trim the fixation nails.

To save the piece, surgery should not be put off too long. A single peeled-off flap of cartilage turned my thinking from non-surgical care to arthroscopic surgery.

One shadow that kept me guessing

There was also a suspicious oval shadow beside the inner edge of the kneecap. Fragments in a dislocation often come from the inner surface of the kneecap, so by location alone it could have been a piece that had broken off. But following it down the slices, it merged into tissue of the same texture and joined the fat pad at the front of the knee (Hoffa’s fat pad). The balance tipped toward a normal extension of the fat pad running up beside the kneecap, but MRI alone could not settle it.

What MRI cannot settle is checked directly with the arthroscope. That is why, at arthroscopy, the inner surface of the kneecap and every corner of the joint deserve a look, not just the outer edge of the thighbone.

Final thoughts

  • Most first-time kneecap dislocations start with non-surgical care, but the risk of recurrence and any cartilage damage should be checked at the outset.
  • Stitching the stretched inner tissue did little to prevent re-dislocation over ten years.
  • There is little reason to “wait until adulthood.” Consider reconstruction if the kneecap dislocates again or the risk is high.
  • If MRI shows a loose piece of cartilage or bone, surgery while the piece can still be saved is the better choice, even after a first dislocation.

If the kneecap slips out again, the knee locks and will not straighten, or swelling and pain get worse, see a doctor promptly.

This article is general medical information. Treatment varies with age, growth plates, the shape of the groove and any cartilage injury, so please confirm with your doctor.
