Bone tumors: age and location are half the diagnosis

Hee Seung Nam, MD, Orthopedic Surgeon · November 27, 2026

“They said there is something like a hole in my bone on the X-ray. Could it be cancer?” It is one of the questions I hear from the most anxious faces in an orthopedic clinic. Fortunately, lesions found in bone are mostly benign. Primary malignant bone tumors, the sarcomas that start in bone itself, are rare enough to make up less than 0.2% of all malignant tumors.

Even so, not missing a rare malignant tumor takes an orderly approach. Before a doctor who treats bone tumors even opens the X-ray, two questions come first: “How old is the patient?” and “Which bone, and where in that bone?” A hole of the same shape makes me think first of osteosarcoma above the knee of a 15-year-old, of giant cell tumor just below the knee joint of a 30-year-old, and of cancer that has spread from another organ in the pelvis of a 65-year-old. It is no exaggeration to say that age and location settle half of the differential diagnosis.

This article walks through bone tumors along those two questions. Alongside what textbooks and papers say, I also share what I found when I analyzed the distribution in real patients.

The first question: how old?

The kinds of bone tumor differ sharply with age. A review of bone tumor imaging in the journal Radiology lists the patient’s age as a key clue alongside location, margins and periosteal reaction. The figure below gives a rough guide to the ages at which each is usually found.

Grouped by age, it looks like this.

Under 10Langerhans cell histiocytosis, simple bone cyst, non-ossifying fibromaMetastatic neuroblastoma, leukemia (primary sarcoma is rare)

40 and overEnchondroma (found incidentally)Metastatic cancer, multiple myeloma, chondrosarcoma, chordoma, secondary osteosarcoma

The most important rule is in the last row. When a bone-destroying lesion first appears after age 40, think of cancer that has spread from another organ, or multiple myeloma, before a primary sarcoma. Bone is one of the organs cancer spreads to most often, and breast, prostate, lung, kidney and thyroid cancers in particular seek out bone.

Conversely, knee pain in a teenager is usually growing pains or a sports injury, but this is also the age at which osteosarcoma and Ewing sarcoma are most common. Osteosarcoma has a first peak during the adolescent growth spurt and a second, smaller peak after 60. In older patients, osteosarcoma often arises in Paget disease of bone or as a second cancer after treatment for another cancer.

A few things stand out.

  • The age order matches the textbooks closely. More than half of Langerhans cell histiocytosis was under 10; osteochondroma, chondroblastoma and simple bone cyst clustered in the teens; giant cell tumor in the 20s and 30s; and chondrosarcoma, metastatic cancer and chordoma after 40.
  • Osteosarcoma was most common in the teens, but more than one in four patients was 40 or older (28%). The share barely falls after the 30s and stays around 10% into the 50s, in line with US population data showing a second peak in older people. The assumption that “osteosarcoma is a children’s disease” is a dangerous one.
  • Only 10% of giant cell tumors were in teenagers. This reflects a tumor that appears after the growth plates close. A similar lesion at the end of a bone in a teenager with open growth plates points first to chondroblastoma.
  • Enchondroma was found most often in people in their 30s and 40s, because it is so often found incidentally on X-rays or MRI taken for another reason.
  • 94% of metastatic cancer was in people 40 or older, and 86% in those 50 or older. Not a single patient was under 20.

Because this tally is based on patients who came for treatment, it may differ from how often these lesions occur in the population. Small benign lesions that need no surgery are undercounted, as are cases such as spinal metastases that are mostly treated by other specialties. Age is at first visit, and percentages are rounded.

The second question: which bone, and where?

Location is read in three steps: first which bone; then, for a long bone, which segment; and finally, in cross-section, whether the lesion is central, off to one side, within the cortex (the outer shell), or on the surface.

The long bones of the limbs are divided, from the end inward, into the epiphysis (the end that forms the joint), the growth plate, the metaphysis (the flared part just beneath the growth plate) and the diaphysis (the central shaft). Each tumor has a favorite segment.

  • Epiphysis: chondroblastoma in a teenager whose growth plate is still open, and giant cell tumor in an adult whose growth plate has closed. So few tumors arise in the epiphysis that this location alone narrows the differential a great deal.
  • Metaphysis: right next to the growth plate, this is where cells divide most actively. Most of the tumors common in teenagers arise here: osteosarcoma, osteochondroma, non-ossifying fibroma, simple bone cyst and aneurysmal bone cyst. They are especially common around the knee (the lower femur and upper tibia), where height grows the most, and in the upper humerus.
  • Diaphysis: Ewing sarcoma, Langerhans cell histiocytosis and fibrous dysplasia favor the shaft. A small lesion inside the cortex suggests osteoid osteoma, and one in the front cortex of the tibia suggests osteofibrous dysplasia or adamantinoma.

Position in cross-section is also a clue. A simple bone cyst sits in the center of the bone; giant cell tumor and non-ossifying fibroma sit off to one side; and osteoid osteoma forms a small nidus within thickened cortex. An osteochondroma grows from the bone surface pointing away from the joint, and parosteal osteosarcoma grows stuck to the bone surface.

Osteosarcoma

The most common primary malignant bone tumor. The area around the knee, the lower femur (34%) and upper tibia (14%) together, accounted for almost half, followed by the pelvis and the upper humerus. Interestingly, the median age of patients with osteosarcoma of the pelvis was 49, far older than around the knee (17). In US population data, too, the sites of osteosarcoma spread beyond the knee as age increases. On X-ray, the hallmarks are an ill-defined lesion in the metaphysis, a sunburst periosteal reaction spreading out from the bone, and the triangle formed where the periosteum is lifted (Codman triangle).

Ewing sarcoma

Most common in the teens, it favors the shaft of long bones and flat bones such as the pelvis, ribs and scapula. Among the patients I analyzed, too, the pelvis was the most common site (20%). Because it can cause fever and raised inflammatory markers, it is sometimes mistaken for bone infection. On X-ray it shows moth-eaten, permeative bone destruction and an onion-skin periosteal reaction stacked in layers, and it often forms a large mass outside the bone.

Chondrosarcoma

Common after 40, it occurred in the pelvis (29%), upper femur (11%) and upper humerus (10%). It favors bones close to the trunk and is rare in the hands and feet. A cartilage tumor inside a long bone can be hard to tell from a benign enchondroma, but pain caused by the tumor, inner cortex scalloped away by more than two-thirds of its thickness, and breach of the cortex point strongly to chondrosarcoma. In a study from the US Armed Forces Institute of Pathology, these criteria told the two tumors apart in at least 90% of cases.

Giant cell tumor

Benign, but it destroys bone as it grows and often recurs, so it is classified as “intermediate.” Typically it sits off to one side, from the epiphysis to just beneath the joint surface, in people aged 20 to 40 whose growth plates have closed. Around the knee, the lower femur (33%) and upper tibia (23%) together made up more than half, with the end of the radius at the wrist third. It is known to be relatively common in Asia, for example in China and southern India.

Osteochondroma

The most common benign bone tumor. Bone sprouts like a mushroom from the surface of the metaphysis near the growth plate, pointing away from the joint. Half were around the knee. It normally grows while a child grows and stops when growth ends. If it grows again or starts to hurt in adulthood, it should be checked for the rare chondrosarcoma arising from its cartilage cap.

Enchondroma

A benign tumor of cartilage left behind inside bone, and the most common bone tumor of the finger bones. More than half (56%) of the patients I analyzed had it in the hand and wrist. In the hand it is often found through a fracture after minor trauma; in the humerus or femur it is often found by chance on knee or shoulder MRI.

Chondroblastoma and simple bone cyst

Chondroblastoma is a rare benign tumor of the epiphysis in teenagers whose growth plates are still open. Besides the knee and upper humerus, it appeared fairly often (16%) in bones that behave like an epiphysis, such as the heel bone and talus. Simple bone cysts are common in the upper femur and upper humerus of children, and are often first found through a fracture after a fall.

Fibrous dysplasia

A lesion in which bone fails to mature normally and is filled with fibrous tissue. It looks hazy, like ground glass, on X-ray, and the upper femur (26%) was the most common site. The femoral neck can bend into a shape like a shepherd’s crook.

Metastatic cancer, multiple myeloma and chordoma

Metastatic cancer was most common in the pelvis (23%) and upper femur (17%). The spine is generally the most common site, but spinal metastases are mostly treated by spine specialists, so they were undercounted in my analysis. Metastases gather in the trunk and the parts of the limbs close to the trunk, and are rare beyond the elbow and knee. Multiple myeloma affects the spine, pelvis, ribs and skull of people in their 50s and 60s and older, and chordoma arises in the sacrum and the base of the skull; in my analysis, too, 72% were in the spine and sacrum.

Putting age and location together

In practice, both questions are weighed together. Here are three hypothetical examples to illustrate.

Age 15 · metaphysis of the lower femur

The area above the knee has hurt for weeks and sometimes wakes him at night. The X-ray shows an ill-defined lesion and a sunburst periosteal reaction. Osteosarcoma is suspected first, and the work-up proceeds to MRI, chest CT and a biopsy at a tumor center.

Age 28 · epiphysis of the upper tibia, off to one side

The area below the knee hurts when climbing stairs. A fairly well-defined lytic lesion reaches just beneath the joint surface. Given an age when the growth plates have closed and this location, giant cell tumor is the first candidate.

Age 65 · upper femur

The groin has ached for months and has recently worsened with walking. A lesion is dissolving the bone. At this age, metastatic cancer or multiple myeloma comes first, and blood tests, chest and abdominal CT and a bone scan are used to search for a primary cancer before biopsy. If the lesion is large and likely to break, surgery to prevent fracture is planned at the same time.

In the third example, removing the lesion without knowing the primary cancer can make treatment much harder in the rare case that it turns out to be a primary sarcoma. That is why even bone lesions after 40 are operated on only after the tests have been done in order.

Pain that should not be dismissed as growing pains

The first symptom of bone sarcoma in a teenager is usually pain. The trouble is that this pain is easily put down to growing pains or a sports injury. In a Swedish study, the time from the first doctor’s visit to the correct diagnosis was 9 weeks for osteosarcoma and 19 weeks for Ewing sarcoma, and 26% of patients with Ewing sarcoma remembered their symptoms starting after a minor injury.

True growing pains usually affect both legs, come in the evening or at night, are felt in the calf and thigh muscles rather than the joints, and are gone by the next morning, when the child runs around normally. The examination is normal. If any of the signs below are present, it may not be growing pains, so start with an X-ray.

Pain that wakes at night or persists at rest

Pain that continues regardless of activity, or bad enough to wake from sleep, needs checking.

One side only, the same spot

Growing pains come and go on both sides. One spot that has hurt for weeks is different.

Swelling or a lump you can feel

If the area around a bone swells or a firm lump can be felt, see a doctor promptly.

A limp, or a fracture from a minor knock

A changed gait, or a bone that breaks with a light impact, may mean a pathologic fracture.

After 40, new bone pain in someone who has been treated for cancer, and back or pelvic pain that comes with unexplained weight loss, deserve the same attention.

Have the biopsy where the surgery will be done

Even when age and location have narrowed the diagnosis, a suspected malignancy is confirmed by biopsy. Tumor cells can be left along the path of the needle or incision, so that path has to be removed together with the tumor at the later operation. The rule, therefore, is that the team that will perform the final surgery plans where the biopsy goes.

In a study of 597 patients by the Musculoskeletal Tumor Society in the United States, biopsies done outside tumor treatment centers led to diagnostic errors and complications 2 to 12 times more often, and there were 18 amputations that could have been avoided. This is why a lump in bone should not be approached with “let’s just take it out and see.”

In closing

  • Most lesions seen in bone are benign. Many, like non-ossifying fibroma in children, can simply be watched without treatment.
  • Age is the first clue. Think first of osteosarcoma and Ewing sarcoma in the teens, of giant cell tumor at the end of a bone in the 20s and 30s, and of metastatic cancer and multiple myeloma after 40.
  • Location is the second clue. Look at which bone, whether epiphysis, metaphysis or diaphysis, and whether the lesion is central or off to one side.
  • Do not dismiss pain with warning signs as growing pains. Pain that wakes at night, pain that stays on one side, swelling or a lump, or a limp calls for an X-ray first.
  • Order matters. Imaging shows the extent, a biopsy planned by the surgical team confirms the diagnosis, and treatment follows.

This article is general medical information. Even at the same age and location, the diagnosis can differ with the imaging findings and symptoms, so if you have been told something abnormal shows on your bone, please confirm it through an examination.
