When a shoulder dislocates in your 70s, why do the tendons tear?

Hee Seung Nam, MD, Orthopedic Surgeon · November 23, 2026

A patient in their 70s slipped on a wet bathroom floor, fell, and came to the hospital with a dislocated right shoulder. I put the shoulder back in place under anesthesia.

In a younger patient, the first thing I would suspect is a tear of the labrum, the rim at the front of the shoulder socket, because that is what most often gets hurt when a shoulder dislocates. This patient's MRI, however, showed injuries to several of the tendons that wrap the shoulder (the rotator cuff), the biceps tendon, and the joint capsule. When I looked inside the joint with an arthroscope, the labrum had not come off the bone; its surface was only slightly frayed.

Same “dislocated shoulder,” very different injury. Why? The answer lies in a weak link that changes with age.

The shoulder is built to dislocate easily

The shoulder joint looks like a golf ball sitting on a tee. The ball of the upper arm bone (the humeral head) is large, but the shallow cup of the shoulder blade that holds it (the glenoid) is small. That lets the arm move in almost every direction, and it also makes the joint easy to dislocate. What the bones cannot hold, the soft tissues do.

  • Labrum: a rim around the shallow glenoid that deepens the cup
  • Capsule and ligaments: the sleeve around the joint and the tough bands within it
  • Rotator cuff: four tendons that press the humeral head into the socket (subscapularis in front, supraspinatus on top, infraspinatus and teres minor behind)

Most shoulder dislocations are anterior dislocations, in which the humeral head pops out forward; they make up about 96% of all shoulder dislocations.1 They usually happen when force hits an arm that is raised out to the side and turned outward, or when you land on your hand or shoulder in a fall.

What tears depends on your age

When the humeral head bursts forward, the weakest of the structures holding the shoulder gives way first.

In younger people the tendons are elastic and tough. They hold, and the front rim, the labrum, is torn off the bone instead. This is called a Bankart lesion. With age, things change. The rotator cuff tendons gradually wear and weaken, and these weakened tendons tear before the rim does.1,2 In older shoulders the tendons are often already partly worn before the injury, so the dislocation can be the final blow.

In older shoulders, the tendons tear along two main routes.

  • The back route: as the humeral head pops forward, it pulls on and tears the top and back tendons (supraspinatus and infraspinatus). In older patients these back structures, already weakened, tear easily.2
  • The front route: the front tendon, the subscapularis, and the capsule are torn off the humerus together. In a study of 37 patients over 40 whose rotator cuff tears were initially missed, all 11 who dislocated again had tears of this front tendon and capsule, and none had a Bankart lesion.3

The subscapularis also acts as a cover over the groove that the biceps tendon runs through. When the subscapularis tears, the biceps tendon can slip inward out of its groove. The MRI of the patient above, showing injuries to the front, top and back tendons, the biceps tendon and the capsule, can be read as both routes happening at once.

How common is this? After age 40, studies report a rotator cuff tear alongside the dislocation in 35–86% of cases, and up to 83% in people who dislocate in their 70s.4 A study that followed patients over 60 for an average of seven years found rotator cuff tears in 61%, and in the same study one in five people with a dislocated shoulder (20%) was 60 or older.5 Shoulder dislocation is not only a young athlete's injury.

Repeat dislocation is less common, but the real problem is a weak shoulder

In younger people, a shoulder that dislocates once often dislocates again: 72–87% around age 20, depending on the study,6 compared with about 21% after age 40.7

So older patients may think, “at least it won't come out again.” But in the older shoulder the problem is not repeat dislocation; it is pain and weakness from torn tendons. People cannot raise the arm above shoulder height, wake up at night from pain, and struggle to wash their hair or reach a shelf.

The bigger problem is that this is often missed. The X-ray taken after the shoulder is put back shows only where the bones are, not the tendons. In the study of 37 patients above, the inability to lift the arm was often wrongly put down to nerve injury rather than a torn tendon.3 A Korean study reported that the later the MRI after a dislocation, the larger the tear and the greater the fatty change in the muscle can become.8

Also check the axillary nerve

When the shoulder dislocates, the axillary nerve, which runs just below the humeral head, can be stretched and injured. This nerve powers the deltoid, the rounded muscle over the shoulder, and supplies feeling to the skin on the outer shoulder. It is injured in about 9–10% of forward dislocations;9 in a study of patients over 60 it was 9.3%, and all of them recovered fully within 3–12 months.5

A dislocation with both a rotator cuff tear and a nerve injury is called the “shoulder terrible triad.”10 Because nerve injury and tendon tear both show up as an arm that “won't work,” one can hide the other, so both need to be checked.

See your doctor again if

If you are over 40, you need your tendons checked if any of the following apply after the shoulder has been put back. One report found that among patients over 40 who still had pain or weakness three weeks after a dislocation, 11 of 13 had a rotator cuff tear.11

  • Three weeks later you still cannot lift the arm above shoulder height by yourself (it goes up if someone lifts it for you)
  • Shoulder pain keeps coming back when you lift the arm or at night
  • The skin on the outer shoulder feels numb, or the rounded shoulder muscle (deltoid) won't tighten
  • The shoulder feels as if it might come out again
Remember“It went back in fine on the X-ray” means the bones are in place, not that the tendons are fine. Tendons are checked with ultrasound or MRI.

Treatment depends on your age, how badly the tendons are torn and how worn they were to begin with, and how much you use your arm. It should be decided in clinic, looking at the imaging and the examination together.

The best treatment is not falling

In older adults, shoulder dislocations usually come from everyday falls, not sports. Bathrooms, where floors stay wet, are especially risky.

  • Put non-slip mats on the bathroom floor and install grab bars next to the toilet and shower.
  • Leave a small light on in the hallway and bathroom for nighttime trips.
  • Wipe up wet floors right away and avoid slippers with slick soles.
  • When you get up from sleep, don't start walking right away; sit for a moment, then stand up slowly.
  • If you also hit your head or face in a fall, get checked for head injury, not just the shoulder, especially if you take blood thinners.

In summary

  • When a shoulder dislocates, younger people mostly injure the front labrum, while people over 40 injure the rotator cuff tendons.
  • Older shoulders rarely dislocate again, but torn tendons often leave pain and weakness.
  • A normal X-ray does not show the tendons. If you still cannot lift the arm by yourself after three weeks, have it checked with ultrasound or MRI.
  • The axillary nerve can be injured at the same time, so both the tendons and the nerve need to be checked.

If you are young, the first question is whether it will dislocate again; if you are over 40, it is whether the tendons are intact. If your shoulder still won't lift three weeks after it was put back, don't wait it out. See your doctor again.

This article is general medical information. Tests and treatment vary with tendon and nerve injury, age and activity level, so please confirm with your doctor.
