No callus on the X-ray at 3 months: checking bone healing with CT

Hee Seung Nam, MD, Orthopedic Surgeon · November 27, 2026 · Three-month follow-up of case 7 from my tibial shaft fracture column

This is a follow-up on the seventh patient in Treatment options for tibial shaft fractures. He is a 40-year-old man who injured his leg on a steel rebar. His shinbone (tibia) was jammed into the smaller calf bone (fibula), an impaction that made it hard to realign the leg in the emergency room. I operated in two stages: first I realigned the leg and fixed the fibula, then ten days later I fixed the tibia with a plate.

The surgery went well, and he was walking with weight on the leg. But on the X-ray taken close to three months after surgery, the white cloud of new bone that usually signals healing (callus) was hard to see. Should we keep waiting, or do something? This column follows that question, and how I answered it by placing the three-month CT next to the CT taken right after surgery.

Bone fixed with a plate heals differently

Broken bone heals in two broad ways. When the fracture moves a little, callus forms on the outside of the bone and bridges the two sides (secondary bone healing). When the fracture surfaces are pressed tightly together, the bone joins directly without callus (primary bone healing). The white cloud we look for on X-rays comes mostly from the first kind.

When a fracture with many pieces is held with a locking plate that spans the broken area like a bridge (bridge plating), things are a little different. The pieces are not each pressed together; the plate simply holds the bone above and below the fracture. The stiffer this fixation, the less the fracture moves, and callus can form later and in smaller amounts. In fractures of the lower thighbone fixed with locking plates, callus has been shown to form unevenly, more on one side than the other.

The location matters too. The lower tibia has relatively more spongy inner bone (cancellous bone) and less hard outer shell (cortex). Cancellous bone tends to heal quietly from the inside rather than building a large callus outside, so the change is easy to miss on X-rays.

Three months is too early to call it "not healing"

Bone that heals more slowly than expected is called a delayed union; bone that has stopped healing is called a nonunion. A nonunion is commonly defined as a fracture that has not healed nine months after injury and has shown no progress over the last three months. A lower tibial fracture fixed with a plate usually takes four to six months to heal, and longer when there are many fragments, as in this patient.

TimeWhat it means
3 months after surgeryIt is common to see little healing on X-rays at this point. I look for anything that slows healing and keep watching.
4–6 monthsMost fractures heal in this window. I check whether the cortex is bridging across the fracture.
After 9 monthsIf there has been no progress for three months, it is treated as a nonunion and other treatment is planned.

So expecting the bone to be fully healed at three months is early. What matters at this point is not whether it has healed, but whether it is moving in the right direction.

What I checked first

When bone is slow to heal, I first look for a reason.

  • Weight bearing: Putting weight on the leg, within what the fixation can hold, is the most reliable stimulus for healing. He was already walking with full weight.
  • Infection: Infection at the surgical site is an important cause of failed healing. His blood marker of inflammation (CRP) was normal, so I judged infection unlikely.
  • General health and habits: I also look for things that slow bone healing, such as smoking, diabetes, anti-inflammatory painkillers and low vitamin D.
  • Plate and screws: I check for signs of screws loosening or the plate bending.

Comparing CT right after surgery and at 3 months

An X-ray is a single shadow of many layers stacked together, so changes hidden behind or overlapping the plate are hard to see. CT shows the bone in thin slices, one at a time, which makes it much easier to tell whether a fracture line has bridged. CT has its own limits, though: the image smears around metal, and it is known to sometimes make a healed fracture look less healed than it is. So I do not judge from one direction alone.

I compared the postoperative CT and the three-month CT at similar levels in three directions. In each video, the left is right after surgery and the right is three months after surgery.

Cross-section (axial)

Right after surgery, the fracture edges were sharp and the pieces were apart. At three months, on many slices the edges had become rounded and blurred, and hazy new bone had started to fill the marrow cavity. This is a sign that the bone is responding. New bone was clearly visible around the fibular plate, so the fibula was healing faster than the tibia. On many slices, however, a dark fracture line was still visible at the edge of the large fragment at the back and outer side.

Viewed from the front (coronal)

In this direction the plate and screws overlap the fracture, so the metal smear is large. Even so, most fracture lines in the cancellous bone had faded as new bone filled them in. Part of the fracture line remained in the cortex opposite the plate and at the edge of the large fragment, and I marked that as the area to watch most closely.

Viewed from the side (sagittal)

The side view is the least affected by metal smear. The fracture lines and gaps between fragments that were clear right after surgery had faded to faint traces at three months, and the front and back cortices looked nearly continuous. This was the best-looking of the three directions, and I think it is the closest to the real state of the bone.

My judgment: healing slowly, not stopped

Putting the three directions together, this fracture has not stopped healing; it is healing slowly. The fracture site is not unresponsive, and nothing suggests infection. The lack of visible callus on the X-ray is most likely explained by the stiff locking-plate fixation and the cancellous bone at the fracture site.

In a study that followed tibial and femoral shaft fractures fixed with intramedullary nails, fractures that showed new bone bridging even one side of the fracture within four months after surgery almost all went on to heal. Plate fixation is a different setting, but for this patient, too, the key is whether more of the cortex has bridged at around four to five months.

Why I decided not to remove the fibular plate

The lower leg has the thick tibia and the thin fibula side by side. If the fibula heals first or is held rigidly by a plate, it can act like a prop (strut) that holds the tibial fracture apart and keeps it from being compressed. Some suggest removing the fibular plate to let more compression reach the tibial fracture. I considered it, but decided against it for this patient.

  • The fibula is already healing: CT showed clear new bone around the fibular plate. A fibula that is already healing keeps acting as a prop even after the plate is removed, so removing it would achieve little.
  • Alignment could be lost: The tibia and fibula broke at almost the same level, so the fibular fixation does a lot to keep the leg straight. Taking away that support while the tibia is not yet healed could let the leg bend inward or outward.
  • The evidence is weak: Reports that fibular fixation slows tibial healing come mostly from tibias fixed with intramedullary nails, and the results are mixed. There is little evidence that removing the plate brings on healing.

The plan from here

TimeWhat to do
NowKeep walking with weight on the leg, and check for anything that slows healing.
In 6–8 weeksX-ray to check the fracture lines and the cortex, and to look for any loosening around the screws or fatigue of the plate.
4–5 monthsIf the picture is still unclear, repeat the CT and compare with this one to see whether more cortex has bridged.
Around 6 monthsCheck whether at least three of the four cortices have bridged. Only if progress has stopped would I consider another operation, such as bone grafting and revision fixation.

Summary

  • Not seeing callus on an X-ray three months after surgery does not mean the bone is not healing.
  • When a multi-fragment fracture is bridged with a locking plate, or when the fracture is in the cancellous lower tibia, callus can appear later and in smaller amounts.
  • A nonunion is diagnosed when a fracture shows no progress for three months, nine months or more after injury. Three months is the time to look for causes and keep watching.
  • When the picture is unclear, comparing CT slices at the same levels as the postoperative CT, in several directions, helps. It is important to include the direction least affected by metal smear.

I hope the next X-ray will show more bridged bone, and I will add his progress to this column. If you have questions, please write to doctornhs@naver.com.
