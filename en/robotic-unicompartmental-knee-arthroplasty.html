Knee

Getting to know robotic partial knee replacement

Hee Seung Nam, MD, Orthopedic Surgeon · November 5, 2026

The knee joint has three compartments: inner (medial), outer (lateral), and behind the kneecap (patellofemoral). A partial knee replacement replaces the cartilage and bone of only one compartment when just that part is worn; it is also called unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA). Unlike a total knee replacement (TKA), which replaces the whole joint, it keeps the healthy parts and ligaments, preserving natural movement as much as possible and allowing faster recovery. It is most often done for medial compartment arthritis, but can also be done for the lateral or patellofemoral compartment.

When is it needed?

  • Arthritis in only one compartment: degenerative or post-traumatic arthritis limited to the inner or outer side of the knee
  • The rest of the joint and the ligaments are healthy: in particular, the anterior cruciate ligament must work properly.
  • Pain not relieved by conservative treatment: medication, injections and physical therapy no longer control the pain and daily life is difficult
  • Relatively young, active patients: well suited to people in their 50s and 60s who are active and want to avoid a total knee replacement.

If arthritis has spread to other compartments or the leg is badly deformed, a partial knee replacement may not be suitable.

Surgery with a robotic arm

I perform partial knee replacement with a robotic arm (MAKO). A 3D plan tailored to the patient's knee is made from a preoperative CT, and during surgery the robotic arm helps cut the bone according to that plan.

  1. Anesthesia: spinal or general anesthesia.
  2. Incision: the knee is opened on the side of the damaged compartment (usually the inner side). The incision is smaller than for a total knee replacement.
  3. Checking the joint: the worn compartment, the other compartments and the anterior and posterior cruciate ligaments are checked again by eye and by hand.
  4. Cutting bone with the robotic arm: only the worn surfaces of the thigh bone and shin bone are cut precisely according to the CT plan.
  5. Trial components and balancing: trial implants are inserted to check alignment, stability and movement.
  6. Fixing the implant and closing: the metal (thigh bone side) and plastic (shin bone side) components are fixed with bone cement and the wound is closed.

The operation usually takes 1 to 2 hours.

A real case

This patient's right knee had cartilage worn away in the medial compartment, with bone rubbing on bone. During surgery we confirmed that the lateral compartment and patellofemoral joint were healthy and that the anterior and posterior cruciate ligaments worked, then replaced only the medial compartment with the robotic arm. At the end of surgery the knee moved smoothly from 0 to 130 degrees.

Recovery and care

  • 1 to 2 weeks after surgery: gradually put weight on the leg using a walker or crutches. Raise the leg to heart level to reduce swelling.
  • 2 to 6 weeks: rehabilitation restores range of motion and strength. Most people return to daily life in this period.
  • After 6 weeks: start light exercise such as walking and swimming; by 3 to 6 months most normal activities are possible.

Manage swelling with ice and compression bandages, and keep strengthening the front thigh muscles (quadriceps). The implant is checked at outpatient visits 6 weeks, 3 months and 1 year after surgery.

Pros and cons

  • Pros: healthy joint and ligaments remain, so the knee moves naturally, and the small incision means faster recovery and a shorter hospital stay. It can be converted to a total knee replacement later if needed.
  • Cons: it is possible only when arthritis is limited to one compartment, and over time the implant may wear or loosen, or arthritis may progress in other compartments, requiring a total knee replacement.

Complications and precautions

Wound infection, leg vein blood clots, implant wear or loosening, and arthritis progressing in other compartments can occur. Medication may be used to prevent clots; smokers should quit and people with excess weight benefit from losing it. Starting rehabilitation early after surgery is also important.

Comparison with total knee replacement

IndicationArthritis in one compartmentArthritis in several compartments, severe deformity
MovementBetter preserves natural movementMay be somewhat limited

DurabilityReported revision rates somewhat higher than total knee replacementOften lasts 15 to 20 years or more
Best suited toRelatively young, active patientsOlder patients, damage in several compartments

A study comparing about 100,000 matched patients from the UK joint registry found that partial knee replacement had fewer complications and deaths after surgery than total knee replacement, but a higher revision rate. It is best to choose with a specialist, considering joint condition, age and activity level.

Returning to daily life

Most people return to daily life within 6 to 12 weeks, and after 3 to 6 months light sports such as golf, swimming and cycling are possible. High-impact activities such as running and jumping can speed up implant wear and are best avoided. Regular rehabilitation exercise and weight control are the keys to making it last.

In closing

A partial knee replacement is a good treatment that can give both pain relief and natural movement to people with only one worn compartment. It is not right for everyone, however, so I recommend deciding after thorough discussion with more than one specialist.
